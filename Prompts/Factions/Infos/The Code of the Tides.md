#### 1. No Slavery, No Chains

_"A man’s will is his own, and the sea respects no master."_

Meaning:

- The Free Captains abhor slavery. Any captain found enslaving others is publicly disgraced, and their crew may abandon them.
- Debt-bondage (where people are "owed" for passage or food) is condemned but tolerated if it’s clearly temporary and consensual.
- Prisoners of war must be freed after a fair trial (usually a crew vote) or ransomed if possible.

Exceptions:

- Some captains kidnap criminals to use as labor (e.g., forcing a murderer to row for their crew as punishment). This is controversial but not unheard of.
- Drowned Ones (the Cult’s undead husks) are sometimes enslaved by the Iron Fleet, but Free Captains hunt their creators rather than keep the undead themselves.

Consequences:

- A captain caught enslaving others may be chased out of the Whirlpool Archipelago or face a trial by combat (though most prefer exile).
- Crew mutinies are common if a captain violates this rule.

Lore Tie-In:

- The Great Drowning (the cataclysm that created the islands) was caused by a tyrant king who enslaved thousands to build floating cities. The Free Captains see themselves as heirs to his victims.

#### 2. Mutual Aid in Storms and Strife

_"Share the wind, share the waves, and share the pain."_

Meaning:

- No crew turns away a drowning sailor. If a ship is sinking, other Free Captains vessels must stop and help (even rivals).
- Storm warnings are shared freely. No captain hoards weather knowledge—it’s a community resource.
- If a crew is attacked by the Iron Fleet or the Pirate King, other Free Captains captains may come to their aid, though they won’t always risk their ships.
- Food and water are often shared among crews during long voyages.

Exceptions:

- If a ship provoked the attack (e.g., raiding a Free Captains ally), aid may be withheld or conditional.
- Some captains charge for rescue, but this is shameful and usually leads to bad reputation.

Consequences:

- A captain who ignores a distress call may be blacklisted from trade in the Whirlpool Archipelago.
- False storm warnings (to sink rivals) are punishable by duel.

Lore Tie-In:

- The Tideborn Monks enforce this rule with their magic. They can summon storms to drown those who refuse aid.
- The Abyssal Maw (a whirlpool leading to the depths) is said to pull down ships of the selfish.

#### 3. No Betrayal Among Crew

_"Trust is the mast that holds a ship upright."_

Meaning:

- Mutiny is rare and severely punished. A captain who betrays their crew (e.g., selling them out, abandoning them, or stealing their wages) forfeits their title.
- Loyalty is earned through time and deeds, not just words. A new crew member must prove themselves before being trusted with secrets.
- Secrets are sacred. A captain’s navigational routes, cargo, or personal grievances are not to be spread, even if the crew disagrees with them.

Exceptions:

- If a captain becomes a threat to the crew’s survival (e.g., leading them into certain doom), mutiny is justified.
- Spies for the Iron Fleet or the Pirate King are hunted down ruthlessly. Turning one in is praised, but accusing someone falsely ruins your reputation forever.

Consequences:

- A betrayer’s ship may be seized, their crew split among rivals, and their name erased from tavern songs.
- Trial by jury of captains is common for serious violations. If found guilty, the captain must face the "Drowning Judgment"—a symbolic execution where they’re thrown into the sea with a weighted sack, but usually pulled back up to live in exile.

Lore Tie-In:

- The first Free Captains captain, Marae, was betrayed by his first mate and cast him into the Maw—his ghost is said to curse traitors with nightmares.
- The compass necklace given to Elias by the Tideborn Monks glows red when someone nearby is lying.

#### 4. Freedom of the Seas, Not Tyranny

_"The wind does not ask for permission to blow, and neither should we."_

Meaning:

- No captain rules absolutely. Even the most powerful captains answer to crew votes for major decisions (e.g., where to sail, whether to fight or flee).
- Women, dwarves, elves, and tieflings are welcomed as crew members and captains. The Captains are not an exclusively human brotherhood.
- No island or port can be "owned" by a single captain. If a crew takes over an island, they must share governance with the locals or risk rebellion.
- Raids on Free Captains allies are forbidden. Stealing from another Free Captains captain can start a blood feud.

Exceptions:

- Temporary command is possible during crises (e.g., a first mate takes over if the captain is injured).
- Debt or favors can create short-term dependencies, but no one can be permanently bound without their consent.

Consequences:

- A captain who tries to enforce slavery or tyranny is deposed immediately.
- Island usurpers may face sieges by local militias or ambushes from rival captains.

Lore Tie-In:

- The Pirate King Veyla Duskbane was once a Free Captains captain before she betrayed the Code and seized Scarlet Isle. The Free Captains still hunt her, but her Red Legion is too strong to defeat alone.
- The original Free Captains were founded by escaped slaves, ex-Iron Fleet deserters, and mercenaries who refused to bow to any king.

### Gray Areas and Debates Within the Free Captains

The Code isn’t black and white—interpretations vary, and debates are common. Some of the biggest arguments include:

1. Should we raid the Iron Fleet’s supply ships?
    - Pro-Code: Stealing from the Iron Fleet hurts the tyrants who enslave people. It’s a form of rebellion.
    - Anti-Code: The Iron Fleet protects islands from monsters and storms. If we raid them, who will defend the weak?
    - _Elias’ stance_: "We raid their corrupt officials, not their kind soldiers. The system is the problem, not the men."
    
2. Can we turn away refugees from Iron Fleet persecution?
    - Pro-Code: The Free Captains must help the persecuted—it’s the heart of the Code.
    - Anti-Code: Too many refugees strain resources, and some may be spies.
    - _Elias’ stance_: "We take them in, but we watch them. The Monks can spot liars."
    
3. Is it okay to sink a rival captain’s ship if they’ve wronged us?
    - Pro-Code: No mercy for those who break trust. Drowning is the sea’s justice.
    - Anti-Code: Murder is murder, even at sea. We should duel, not assassinate.
    - _Elias’ stance_: "I’ve sunk ships in my past, and it haunts me. We take revenge with strategy, not slaughter."
    
4. Should we ally with the Red Legion to fight the Iron Fleet?
    - Pro-Code: The Iron Fleet is the greater evil. We must unite against them.
    - Anti-Code: The Red Legion is worse. They’d enslave us in the end.
    - _Elias’ stance_: "We fight them indirectly—sabotage, deception, and stealing their strength. Never openly."

### How the Code Affects Your Campaign

1. Moral Dilemmas for the Players:
    
    - Should they help a drowning Iron Fleet soldier (breaking the Code’s "no aid to tyrants" rule)?
    - Should they steal from a Free Sail ally to survive, even if it’s against the Code?
    - Should they turn in a crewmate who’s secretly a Cultist, or keep the peace among the crew?

2. Reputation System:
    - The players will gain or lose standing among Free Captains captains based on their actions. This could open or close doors (e.g., only high-rep crews can trade with certain islands).
    - Tavern songs will spread rumors of their deeds—are they heroes, villains, or something in between?

3. Captain Elias’ Leadership Style:
    - Elias respects the Code but isn’t dogmatic. He’ll argue with the crew over tactics, showing that even leaders debate ethics.
    - He might secretly break the Code in extreme cases (e.g., sacrificing a rival to save his crew), creating internal conflict.

4. Faction Tensions:
    - The Iron Fleet sees the Code as lawlessness, using it to justify their raids.
    - The Red Legion mocks it as weakness, while the Drowned God’s Cult wants to corrupt it (e.g., convincing Free Captains to drown their own to "prove loyalty").
    - The Tideborn Monks are the unofficial enforcers of the Code, using their magic to curse those who break it.

### Examples of the Code in Action

1. The Trial of Captain Harkin:
    
    - Harkin was accused of enslaving a fisherman to crew his ship. At her trial, the fisherman refused to testify against her, revealing she’d freed him after a month (the "debt" was actually protection during a storm).
    - The council acquitted her, but her reputation was tainted. Now, she’s forced to take on a "penance crew"—former slaves who help her prove her good faith.
2. The Storm That Never Was:
    
    - Captain Ryn claimed a massive storm was coming to trap her rivals. When the Free Captains didn’t respond, the storm actually hit—but it was unnaturally fierce, and many blamed Ryn for summoning it with dark magic.
    - The Tideborn Monks investigated and found she was telling the truth, but the damage was done. She now sails with a Monk’s blessing to prove her honesty.
3. The Betrayer’s End:
    
    - First Mate Garrik stole the crew’s wages and fled with an Iron Fleet officer. The captain let him go—not to show mercy, but to let the sea judge him.
    - Garrik’s ship was lost in the Abyssal Maw, and rumors say his ghost haunts the Whirlpool, a warning to future traitors.

### Breaking the Code: Consequences

|Violation|Consequence|
|---|---|
|Slavery or Debt-Bondage|Exile from the Whirlpool, crew mutiny, or trial by combat.|
|Refusing Aid to a Drowning Crew|Blacklisted from trade, storms summoned by the Monks.|
|Betraying Your Crew|Drowning Judgment, ship seized, name erased from tavern songs.|
|Raiding a Free Sail Ally|Blood feud, siege by rival crews, or "accidental" shipwrecks.|
|Enslaving a Child|Public execution (thrown into the sea with weights).|
|Stealing from a Fellow Captain|Duel to the death (first blood = forgiveness; second blood = death).|
|Lying to the Tideborn Monks|Your compass (or equivalent) stops working; storms follow you.|

### Code-Inspired NPCs for Your Campaign

1. Elder Maris the Stormborn:
    
    - A centenarian half-elf who recites the Code like scripture.
    - She runs the Great Council and has the power to banish captains.
    - Secret: She’s partially undead, gifted with immortality by the Tideborn Monks.
2. Sergeant Dain of the Black Brigade:
    
    - A disgraced Iron Fleet officer who now sails with the Free Captains under a false name.
    - He hates the Code but respects its spirit. He argues with Elias about how far to go in rebellion.
3. Lady Veyla’s Spy, "Silas the Snake":
    
    - A charming tiefling who poses as a Free Sail ally but reports to the Red Legion.
    - He knows the Code’s loopholes and uses them to infiltrate crews.
4. Brother Kael of the Tideborn:
    
    - A monk who tests captains by sending them into storms alone.
    - If they survive, he blesses them; if they drown, he mourns them and curses their betrayers.

### How to Use the Code in Your Game

- First Session Teaser: During the inn brawl, the Iron Fleet sergeant might laugh at the Free Captains Code, calling it "naïve". This could inspire the players to defend it or debate its weaknesses.
- Crew Loyalty Mechanics: The players’ new crewmates (recruited on their first voyage) might respect Elias’ leadership but question his past. Do they trust him completely, or wait to see his next move?
- Moral Choices:
    - Should they sink an Iron Fleet ship carrying supplies (hurting civilians) or let it pass?
    - Should they exile a crewmate for a past crime, or give them a chance to redeem themselves?
- Faction Diplomacy: The Free Captains’ elders might task the players with negotiating with a disgruntled island or a rogue captain, forcing them to use the Code as leverage.

### Final Thought: The Code’s Symbolism

The Code of the Tides represents freedom with responsibility—a delicate balance that defines the Free Captains. It’s:

- An ideal that some strive to uphold.
- A weapon that others exploit (e.g., the Red Legion twists it to justify tyranny).
- A myth that the Cult seeks to corrupt (e.g., convincing pirates to drown their own to "prove their loyalty to the sea").

For your players, it’ll be a guiding philosophy—one that shapes their adventures, alliances, and conflicts. Will they follow it strictly, or bend it to survive? And who will judge them when they do?
